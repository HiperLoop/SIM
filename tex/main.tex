\section{Introduction}\label{intro}
    \subsection{The Ising model}
        The Ising model named after the German physicist Erns Ising is a model in statistical mechanics. The model consists of discrete magnetic spin dipole moments that take values $\pm 1$ ("up" and "down" respectively) arranged on a lattice. For the purposes of our simulation we used a square lattice. The spins interact with neighbouring spins. The model exhibits phase transitions that can be associated with the gas-liquid transition or spontaneous magnetisation.
    \subsection{Aim of study}
        In this article we look at determining at what temperature does the Ising model undergo a phase transition. It is know from theory that under a certain critical temperature the model stabilises into completely up or down state. Conversely, above this critical, on average, the model remains a random mixture of up and down spins.
    
\section{Theory}\label{theory}
    The theoretical value of the critical temperature was predicted by Lars Onsager\cite{Onsager1944} to be
    \begin{equation}
        T_c = \frac{2}{\ln(1 + \sqrt{2})}. \label{critical_temperature}
    \end{equation}
    The interaction energy between two spins is given by
    \begin{equation}
        E_{\text{int}} = -Js_is_j,
    \end{equation}
    where $J>0$ is the coupling constant fo ferromagnetic interractions and the $s_i$ being the value of spin of spin $i$.\\
    We define the reduced interaction energy as $E_r = \frac{E_{\text{int}}}{J} = -s_is_j$.
    The reduced interaction energy with the $j$ nearest neighbours is then
    \begin{equation}
        E_r = \sum_{\braket{j}}s_is_j, \label{neighbour_interaction_energy}
    \end{equation}

\section{Methodology}
    \subsection{Metropolis algorithm}
        To simulate the evolution of the Ising model we use a Monte Carlo simulation using the Metropolis algorithm. In our implementation we consider the von Neumann neighbourgood of only 4 neighbouring spins in the $\pm x$ and $\pm y$ directions. We also use a periodic boundary condition such that if we denote by $n \cross n$ the size of the lattice, we have that the spin at position $(n, i)$ is a neighbor of spin at postion $(1, i)$ and in the same way also spin at $i, n$ is neighbouring the spin at $(i, 1)$. At each iteration of the algorithm a random position is chosen in the square lattice of spins. The energy needed to flip the spin form the up to the down state or vice versa is then calculated from Eq. (\ref{neighbour_interaction_energy}) as
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta E = E_{\text{before}} - E_{\text{after}} = \sum_{\braket{j}}(-s_i)s_j - \sum_{\braket{j}}s_is_j = -2\sum_{\braket{j}}s_is_j. \label{spin_flip_energy_delta}
        \end{equation}
        We then have 2 cases. If $\Delta E \le 0$, we flip the spin orientation. If $\Delta E \ge 0$, we accept the flip with probability $p = e^{\frac{\Delta E}{T_r}}$. In order to do this, we sample a random variable $X \sim Unif(0, 1)$ and check $X < p$, in which case we accept the flip and leave the spin unflipped otherwise.\\
        We knwo that for values of the reduced temperature below the critical temperature i.e. $T_r < T_c$ the system gets to an equilibrium state where the absolute value of the averege magnetisation goes to 1\cite{Blundell2010}.
